Gold: West African goldfields helped sustain long-distance trade. Selected examples include Bambuk and Bure, as well as modern Ghana’s Ashanti region.

Petroleum: Modern oilfields and exploration regions include Ghana’s offshore basin, the Niger Delta, Algeria, Libya, and Angola.

Discovery is not certain: New deposits are assessed through geological mapping, geophysical surveys, drilling, and economic evaluation.

These are modern locations, not evidence that medieval empires knew of or extracted petroleum. Their borders and dates varied; the shaded areas are broad historical approximations.

The historical lens

Three influential West African states

Their fortunes were shaped by trade networks, political authority, scholarship, and many other forces—not by modern oil extraction.

Ghana (Wagadu)

c. 700–1200 CE

An early Sahelian power, centered north of the modern state of Ghana. It grew influential through control of regional trade, including the trans-Saharan exchange in gold and salt.

Mali

c. 1235–1600 CE

A large empire in the western Sahel. Under rulers including Mansa Musa, its cities and trans-Saharan connections linked communities, scholarship, and commerce across a wide region.

Songhai

c. 1464–1591 CE

Based around Gao and the Niger bend, Songhai became a major power in the Sahel and controlled important river and trans-Saharan trade routes before its decline in the late sixteenth century.

Senegal

Upper Senegal River · Historic gold trade

The Bambuk gold-producing zone is associated with the upper Senegal River basin, a broad region that crosses today's borders. Gold from this western Sahel network moved through regional markets and trans-Saharan trade; the map's marker is not a modern mine boundary.

Senegal's modern geology is varied, and mineral exploration is distinct from the medieval gold trade. Any new discovery would require field evidence, testing, permits, and economic review.

Mali

Empire heartland · Niger River · Gold

Parts of the Mali Empire and later Songhai lay within modern Mali, though both empires extended across changing borders. The Niger River connected cities such as Timbuktu and Gao to trading networks. Gold helped sustain commerce, alongside salt, agriculture, scholarship, and political power.

Modern gold mines and exploration areas in western and southern Mali belong to a different era and economy. Their locations do not show that medieval rulers mined the same deposits or controlled today's national territory.

Guinea

Upper Niger · Bure goldfield

Guinea lies in a region linked to the Bure goldfields and the headwaters of major West African rivers. Northeast Guinea also has modern gold-mining districts. These historical and modern locations are regional reference points, not boundaries of a single continuous orebody.

Guinea is also known for major bauxite resources. Different minerals form under different geological conditions, so the presence of gold does not by itself predict oil or another deposit nearby.

Ghana

Ashanti gold belt · Gulf of Guinea petroleum

Important distinction: modern Ghana is not the location of the medieval Ghana Empire (Wagadu), which was farther north. The modern country's gold industry is associated with mineralized belts including the Ashanti region.

Ghana also produces oil and gas from offshore fields in the Gulf of Guinea, including the Jubilee field. These are separate geological settings: inland gold deposits are not evidence of offshore petroleum, and neither should be projected onto historical empire borders.

Nigeria

Niger Delta · Oil and natural gas

Nigeria's petroleum industry is concentrated in the Niger Delta and adjoining offshore areas. Oil was discovered near Oloibiri in 1956, before production and infrastructure expanded. The Delta is a large wetland and sedimentary basin, not one single oilfield.

Petroleum revenue has been economically significant, while spills and other impacts have raised long-running concerns for wetlands, livelihoods, and water quality. Resource development brings environmental and governance responsibilities as well as income.

Algeria

Sahara · Hydrocarbon basins

Algeria's oil and natural gas fields are found mainly in Saharan sedimentary basins. Hassi Messaoud is a major oil field and Hassi R'Mel a major gas field; both illustrate why basin geology and exploration history matter more than a simple surface map.

Much of the country is arid desert. Water use, infrastructure, transport, and the environmental footprint of extraction are important considerations alongside the resource itself.

Libya

Sirte Basin · Petroleum

Libya's petroleum resources are strongly associated with the Sirte Basin and other sedimentary basins. Discoveries beginning in the late 1950s led to oil production becoming central to the modern economy. The map shows a broad region rather than a surveyed field boundary.

Petroleum potential depends on buried source rocks, reservoir quality, seals, and preservation. Geology may indicate prospectivity, but only exploration and evaluation can establish a commercial accumulation.

Read the U.S. EIA Libya profile ↗

Libya and neighboring countries · generalized boundaries

Angola

Atlantic margin · Offshore petroleum

Angola's petroleum sector is closely tied to offshore Atlantic sedimentary basins, including deep-water areas. Offshore discoveries require specialized seismic surveys, drilling technology, and substantial investment; a marker on this map cannot indicate depth, reserves, or commercial viability.

The location of this modern industry is unrelated to the medieval empires of the western Sahel. Country-level comparisons help show how Africa's resource settings vary widely.

Read the U.S. EIA Angola profile ↗

Angola and neighboring countries · generalized boundaries

South Africa

Witwatersrand Basin · Gold

The Witwatersrand Basin, near Johannesburg, hosts a famous gold-bearing reef system. Gold discoveries in the 1880s helped drive rapid industrial development, urban growth, migration, and profound political and social change.

Much of the ore is deep underground, making extraction technically demanding. The history also highlights worker safety, mine waste, water management, and the long-term effects of mining on communities and landscapes.
